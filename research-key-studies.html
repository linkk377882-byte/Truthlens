Key Studies

The Scoping of Misinformation Research (2016-2022)

Author: Broda et al., 2024

Summary: Misinformation, disinformation, and fake news are not new, but they gained much more academic attention after events such as Brexit and the 2016 U.S. presidential election. This review examines 1,261 journal articles published between 2010 and 2021 and finds that most studies focus on spread, detection, and media effects, while still leaving major gaps in cross-field synthesis and future research direction.

Multimodal Analysis of Disinformation and Misinformation

Author: Wilson et al., 2023

Summary: This review brings together research on language, images, sounds, and gestures instead of treating misinformation as text-only communication. It shows how multimodal analysis accelerated after 2020, especially in response to COVID-19, and argues that future work must better connect computer science, linguistics, and communication studies to understand how synthetic narratives persuade people across different media formats.

Thinking Clearly about Misinformation

Author: Tay et al., 2024

Summary: The authors frame misinformation as a public-health problem and organize possible responses into three levels of prevention. Tertiary prevention focuses on moderation, corrections, and warning labels after harmful content circulates; secondary prevention aims to nudge users and improve media literacy; primary prevention asks for systemic changes in platform design and regulation before false narratives spread.

The Science of Fake News

Author: Lazer et al., 2018

Summary: This foundational article explains why people believe fake news, how it travels across social networks, and why traditional safeguards against misinformation weakened in the internet era. It also highlights open scientific questions about exposure, amplification, and social vulnerability, especially in the context of modern political communication.

Social Media and the Spread of Misinformation: Infectious and a Threat to Public Health

Author: Denniss et al., 2025

Summary: This article argues that misinformation behaves like an infectious threat to public health because social platforms enable rapid exposure, repetition, and emotional contagion. It shows how non-expert virality, algorithmic amplification, and delayed interventions can undermine trust in institutions and calls for layered prevention responses across governments, health systems, and technology companies.

(Why) Is Misinformation a Problem?

Author: Adams et al., 2023

Summary: Rather than assuming misinformation is harmful only because of digital technology, the authors ask why it is considered a democratic problem in the first place. They challenge technology-centric explanations, question whether new media are always the main cause of harm, and argue that some anti-misinformation responses can create trade-offs for democratic norms and free expression.
